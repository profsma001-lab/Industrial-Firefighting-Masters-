## Slide 03 · What Fire Dynamics Actually Studies

**Section:** 01 · INTRODUCTION TO FIRE DYNAMICS  
**Subtitle:** The physics and chemistry of fire, and of fire in an enclosure  
**Timecode:** 01:14 · 39 s


**On screen**

- Ignition and the conditions for sustained burning
- The rate at which fuel releases energy
- How that energy moves by conduction, convection and radiation
- How smoke and hot gases move, stratify and fill a space
- How the building, its ventilation and its contents feed back on the fire


**Narration**

> Fire dynamics is the study of fire as a physical and chemical phenomenon. It asks four questions. First, how does a material begin to burn, and what keeps it burning? Second, at what rate is energy released? Third, how does that energy travel to other objects? And fourth, how do the products of combustion, the smoke and the hot gases, move through the space and change the fire itself? Fire dynamics is therefore a system science. The flame, the fuel, the air and the building all influence one another. Change one of them, and the fire changes.


**Teaching note:** Emphasise feedback: the enclosure is part of the combustion system, not a passive container.

## Slide 04 · Why Industrial Firefighting Demands This Knowledge

**Section:** 01 · INTRODUCTION TO FIRE DYNAMICS  
**Subtitle:** Scale, fuel chemistry, process equipment and escalation  
**Timecode:** 01:54 · 42 s


**On screen**

- Industrial fuel loads are dense, mixed and often reactive.
- Open volumes delay flashover but produce enormous smoke production.
- Process equipment introduces pressure, chemicals and explosion hazard.
- Escalation, not the first flame, is the real threat.


**Narration**

> Industrial firefighting is not domestic firefighting at a larger scale. The physical regimes differ. A dwelling gives us a small compartment, a predictable fuel load, and flashover within five to ten minutes. A warehouse, a refinery or a manufacturing hall gives us enormous volumes, dense and chemically varied fuel, and process equipment that can fail under fire exposure. In an industrial setting the fire may never flash over in the domestic sense, yet it can release hundreds of megawatts, destroy structural steel, and escalate from a single ignition point to a site-wide emergency. That is why this course exists.

## Slide 06 · Fire, Combustion and Flame

**Section:** 02 · DEFINITIONS  
**Subtitle:** Three words that are not interchangeable  
**Timecode:** 03:23 · 48 s


**Narration**

> Let us be precise from the start, because these three words are often used loosely. Combustion is a self-sustaining exothermic chemical reaction, usually between a fuel and oxygen, that releases heat and light. Self-sustaining is the key phrase: the reaction supplies the energy needed to keep itself going. A flame is the visible, luminous zone of that reaction in the gas phase, where fuel vapour and oxygen meet and burn. And fire is the whole phenomenon: the combustion, the flame, the heat, the smoke, the toxic gases and their effects on people and structures. So a material can combust without a visible flame, as in smouldering charcoal, and a fire can exist as an entire system.


**Teaching note:** Insist on the distinction: smouldering combustion has no flame.

## Slide 07 · Heat, Temperature and Thermal Energy

**Section:** 02 · DEFINITIONS  
**Subtitle:** The most common conceptual error in firefighting  
**Timecode:** 04:11 · 44 s


**On screen**

- Temperature tells you how hot; heat flux tells you how dangerous.
- A steel beam at 500 °C stores far more heat than a spark at 1 500 °C.
- Ignition depends on heat flux and time, not temperature alone.
- Water absorbs heat, not temperature.


**Narration**

> Temperature and heat are not the same thing. Temperature measures how hot a body is, effectively the average kinetic energy of its molecules. Heat is a quantity of energy, measured in joules. Heat flux is the rate at which heat arrives at a surface, in kilowatts per square metre, and it is heat flux, together with exposure time, that determines whether something ignites. A spark at fifteen hundred degrees carries almost no energy, while a steel column at five hundred degrees stores megajoules. This distinction explains a practical fact: water cools because it absorbs heat, roughly two point four megajoules per kilogram when it turns to steam, not because it lowers temperature directly.


**Teaching note:** Use the spark versus beam analogy; it lands well with practitioners.

## Slide 08 · Heat Release Rate: The Master Variable

**Section:** 02 · DEFINITIONS  
**Subtitle:** Energy per second, and the number that governs everything else  
**Timecode:** 04:56 · 45 s


**On screen**

- 1 kg of wood ≈ 16 MJ; 1 kg of petrol ≈ 44 MJ.
- A fire is characterised by its HRR curve, not by its size.
- HRR drives temperature, smoke production, flame length and heat flux.
- Design and tactics are both sized to HRR.


**Narration**

> The heat release rate, written Q-dot and measured in kilowatts, is the single most important quantity in fire dynamics. It is simply the mass of fuel burning each second, multiplied by the energy released per kilogram of fuel, multiplied by a combustion efficiency that accounts for incomplete burning. One kilogram of wood releases about sixteen megajoules; one kilogram of petrol, about forty-four. Everything else we care about follows from the heat release rate: the temperature of the hot layer, the volume of smoke, the length of the flames, the heat flux to a neighbouring building, and the water flow required to control it. If you know the heat release rate, you can estimate the rest. If you do not, you are estimating blind.


**Teaching note:** Signpost that HRR returns in section 7 with real values.

## Slide 09 · From the Triangle to the Tetrahedron

**Section:** 03 · THE FIRE TETRAHEDRON  
**Subtitle:** Four elements must be present together for flaming combustion  
**Timecode:** 05:42 · 48 s


**Narration**

> For most of the twentieth century we taught the fire triangle: fuel, oxygen and heat. The triangle explained smouldering and simple burning, and it still explains why we cool, smother and starve a fire. But it could not explain why some fires continued to burn in conditions where fuel, oxygen and heat were all present. The answer is the fourth element: the chemical chain reaction. Flaming combustion proceeds through fast radical reactions, in which highly reactive fragments such as hydrogen and hydroxyl radicals propagate the flame. Add that fourth element and the triangle becomes a tetrahedron: fuel, oxygen, heat and chain reaction, each one a face, and all four required at once.


**Teaching note:** Explain radical chain branching simply: molecular fragments that keep the reaction going.

## Slide 10 · Fuel: What Actually Burns

**Section:** 03 · THE FIRE TETRAHEDRON  
**Subtitle:** Almost all fuels burn as gases or vapours, not as solids  
**Timecode:** 06:30 · 49 s


**On screen**

- Solids pyrolyse: heat breaks them into flammable vapours.
- Liquids burn as their vapour above the surface.
- Gases are already fuel; they only need mixing.
- Industrial fuels: polymers, hydrocarbons, dusts, metals, cable compounds.


**Narration**

> Here is a point that surprises many recruits: with very few exceptions, solids and liquids do not burn. What burns is the gas above them. A log of wood is heated until pyrolysis breaks its polymers into flammable vapours, and those vapours burn. Petrol in a tray is not on fire; the vapour layer above the liquid is. This is why a liquid below its flash point is relatively safe, and why a solid that chars can keep producing fuel long after the flame is out. In industry the fuels are polymers such as polyethylene and polyurethane, hydrocarbons, combustible dusts, and in special cases metals. Each behaves differently, and each produces different smoke.


**Teaching note:** Pyrolysis is the concept to stress. Link forward to flash point.

## Slide 11 · Oxygen, Oxidisers and Radical Inhibition

**Section:** 03 · THE FIRE TETRAHEDRON  
**Subtitle:** Air is 21 per cent oxygen; combustion needs far less, and warns us at both extremes  
**Timecode:** 07:19 · 52 s


**On screen**

- Flames generally extinguish below 14–15 % oxygen.
- Smouldering can persist at 5–8 % oxygen.
- Oxygen-enriched atmospheres make materials burn violently.
- Oxidisers such as nitrates and peroxides supply their own oxygen.


**Narration**

> Air contains twenty-one per cent oxygen by volume. Flaming combustion typically cannot be sustained below about fourteen or fifteen per cent, but smouldering can continue at five to eight per cent, which matters in deep-seated and silo fires. At the other extreme, an oxygen-enriched atmosphere, above about twenty-three and a half per cent, dramatically lowers ignition energy and accelerates burning; clothing and hair can burn fiercely. And we must remember oxidisers: nitrates, chlorates, peroxides and ammonium nitrate contain oxygen within their own structure. They can sustain or intensify combustion where air is excluded, which is why storage segregation rules exist. The fourth element, the chain reaction, is the target of clean agents and dry chemical powders: they interrupt the radical chemistry rather than cooling or smothering.


**Teaching note:** Mention Beirut 2020 only as a storage-hazard example, not as a fire dynamics case.

## Slide 12 · Three Modes, Always Simultaneous

**Section:** 04 · HEAT TRANSFER  
**Subtitle:** Conduction, convection and radiation move the fire through the building  
**Timecode:** 08:12 · 47 s


**Narration**

> Heat moves in three ways, and in a real fire all three operate at once. Conduction is transfer through a solid by molecular contact. Convection is transfer by the movement of a fluid, in our case hot smoke and gases. Radiation is transfer by electromagnetic waves, requiring no medium at all. As a fire develops, the dominant mode changes. In the early stage, convection in the plume and radiation from the flame dominate. In the growth stage, radiation from the deepening hot layer becomes decisive. In a fully developed compartment fire, radiation and convection through the openings account for most of the energy leaving the compartment. Understanding which mode dominates tells you what to cool, and where.


**Teaching note:** Set up the three following slides.

## Slide 13 · Conduction: The Hidden Path

**Section:** 04 · HEAT TRANSFER  
**Subtitle:** Fourier's law, and why fire appears where there is no flame  
**Timecode:** 08:59 · 48 s


**On screen**

- q = k A ΔT / L — heat flow rises with area and temperature difference, falls with thickness.
- Steel (k ≈ 45) conducts a thousand times better than mineral wool (k ≈ 0.04).
- Conduction ignites roofs, spreads along pipes, cable trays and steel beams.
- Contact cooling with water interrupts the path.


**Narration**

> Conduction is described by Fourier's law: heat flow equals the thermal conductivity of the material, times the area, times the temperature difference, divided by the thickness. The conductivity constant matters enormously. Steel conducts heat at about forty-five watts per metre kelvin, mineral wool at about zero point zero four. That is a factor of a thousand. Conduction is why a fire in a room can ignite material in the roof space above it, why heat travels along a steel pipe through a fire wall, and why cable trays and conveyor belts carry fire from one end of a plant to the other. It is also why a closed steel door is not a fire barrier unless it is insulated: the door leaf stays cool on the fire side and hot on the other.


**Teaching note:** Concrete example: steel door with uninsulated leaf; heat conducted to the non-fire side.

## Slide 14 · Convection: Smoke Is the Delivery System

**Section:** 04 · HEAT TRANSFER  
**Subtitle:** Newton's law of cooling, and the buoyant transport of energy  
**Timecode:** 09:48 · 49 s


**On screen**

- q = h A ΔT — heat carried by a moving fluid.
- Hot gases expand, become buoyant and rise.
- Most fire energy leaves the compartment through openings as hot gas.
- Smoke movement, not flame spread, is usually how fire reaches a new floor.


**Narration**

> Convection is heat carried by a moving fluid. Newton's law of cooling expresses it simply: heat flow equals a convective coefficient, times the area, times the temperature difference between the surface and the gas. In fire, convection is dominated by buoyancy. Hot products of combustion are less dense than the surrounding air, so they rise, entraining air as they go, and form a plume. That plume strikes the ceiling and spreads as a hot layer. Where that layer finds a stair, a shaft, a duct or a roof void, it carries heat and toxic products to places the flame never reached. In a compartment fire, the majority of energy leaves through the openings in the form of hot gas. This is why we say that smoke is the conveyor belt of the fire.


**Teaching note:** Link convection to smoke control strategy later.

## Slide 15 · Radiation: The Distance Threat

**Section:** 04 · HEAT TRANSFER  
**Subtitle:** Stefan–Boltzmann law: radiative power grows with the fourth power of temperature  
**Timecode:** 10:37 · 54 s


**On screen**

- q″ = ε σ (T₁⁴ − T₂⁴), with temperature in kelvin.
- Double the absolute temperature: sixteen times the radiative power.
- Radiant heat travels line of sight: it ignites what it can see.
- At 20 kW/m², protected skin reaches pain in about 5 s; bare skin blisters in seconds.


**Narration**

> Radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves, and it needs no medium. The Stefan-Boltzmann law tells us that radiative power is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature. That fourth power is why fire is so non-linear. Double the absolute temperature of a surface and it radiates sixteen times as much energy. It also explains why radiant heat is a line-of-sight threat: it ignites the surfaces it can see, and a simple barrier eliminates it. For people, the thresholds are brutal. At two and a half kilowatts per square metre we can work for a short time. At twenty kilowatts per square metre, protected skin gives pain in about five seconds and unprotected skin blisters in seconds. Flashover is defined partly by reaching that figure at floor level.


**Teaching note:** Numbers to memorise: 2.5, 5, 20 kW/m².

## Slide 16 · Reading a Fire Through Its Heat Paths

**Section:** 04 · HEAT TRANSFER  
**Subtitle:** A single burning pallet, traced through three modes  
**Timecode:** 11:32 · 52 s


**On screen**

- Plume convects heat to the ceiling; the layer radiates down.
- Steel rack conducts heat into the next tier of storage.
- Radiation preheats the neighbouring rack; ignition follows.
- Each path suggests a different intervention.


**Narration**

> Let us trace a single burning pallet in a warehouse. The flame convects heat upward, the ceiling jet spreads it laterally, and within a minute a hot layer is forming over the whole aisle. That layer radiates downward onto the pallets below, preheating them without any flame contact. At the same time the steel upright of the rack conducts heat into the next tier, and radiant heat from the flame preheats the rack opposite. Within a few minutes the second rack ignites, and now we have two fire sources entraining air from the same aisle. Each heat path points to a different intervention: cool the layer, cool the steel, cool the exposure, or control the air. Good tactics cool the path that is actually driving the fire, not simply the flame that is visible.


**Teaching note:** This slide is the tactical hinge of section 4.

## Slide 17 · Ignition Temperature and Piloted Ignition

**Section:** 05 · IGNITION PARAMETERS  
**Subtitle:** The surface temperature at which sustained flaming begins  
**Timecode:** 12:25 · 48 s


**On screen**

- Piloted ignition: a spark or flame provides the activation energy.
- Autoignition: the material self-heats until it ignites without a pilot.
- Ignition temperature falls as exposure time and heat flux rise.
- Thin, low-density fuels ignite faster: ratio of area to mass.


**Narration**

> Ignition temperature is the temperature at which a material produces flammable vapours fast enough to sustain flaming combustion. There are two routes. In piloted ignition, an external source, a spark or a small flame, provides the activation energy at the moment the fuel-air mixture is within its flammable range. In autoignition, no pilot is needed: the material is heated until it self-ignites. In practice, ignition temperature is not a single fixed number. It depends on the heat flux, on how long the exposure lasts, and on the material's thermal inertia. Thin materials with a high surface-area-to-mass ratio, such as paper, cotton waste or polymer film, heat through quickly and ignite at lower temperatures than thick timber.


**Teaching note:** Introduce thermal inertia qualitatively: k-rho-c.

## Slide 18 · Autoignition Temperature

**Section:** 05 · IGNITION PARAMETERS  
**Subtitle:** Why oily rags and lagging catch fire without a spark  
**Timecode:** 13:14 · 53 s


**On screen**

- AIT is a property of the material and its geometry, not a constant.
- Self-heating: slow oxidation generates heat faster than it is lost.
- Classic industrial causes: oil-soaked lagging, paint rags, coal, hay, dust layers.
- Hot work near combustible insulation is a leading ignition source.


**Narration**

> Autoignition temperature is the temperature at which a material ignites spontaneously in air, without an external ignition source. Typical values are around two hundred and fifty to three hundred degrees for wood, about five hundred and forty for methane, and about five hundred and eighty-five for hydrogen. But the practically important mechanism is self-heating. A material that oxidises slowly, such as oil-soaked lagging, a pile of paint rags or a coal stockpile, generates heat throughout its volume while losing heat only from its surface. If the pile is large enough and insulated well enough, the core temperature climbs until autoignition occurs. This is the mechanism behind many fires attributed to 'spontaneous combustion', and it is why hot work near combustible insulation, and the storage of oily waste, are controlled so strictly.


**Teaching note:** Distinguish AIT from self-heating; state that self-heating is size-dependent.

## Slide 19 · Flash Point, Fire Point and Flammable Range

**Section:** 05 · IGNITION PARAMETERS  
**Subtitle:** The language of liquid fuel hazard  
**Timecode:** 14:07 · 48 s


**On screen**

- Flash point: lowest temperature at which a vapour ignites momentarily.
- Fire point: temperature at which vapour burns continuously, usually 5–20 °C above flash point.
- Flammable range: between the lower and upper explosive limits.
- Below the flash point the liquid is, in practice, not a fire risk.


**Narration**

> For liquids we use three linked concepts. The flash point is the lowest temperature at which the liquid gives off enough vapour to form an ignitable mixture, which flashes when a pilot is applied. The fire point is a few degrees higher, the temperature at which the vapour continues to burn once ignited. And the flammable range is the range of vapour concentration in air between the lower and upper explosive limits, within which ignition produces a flame or an explosion. Petrol has a flash point around minus forty-three degrees, so it is always producing flammable vapour at ambient temperature. Diesel has a flash point near sixty degrees, so in a temperate climate a spill is far less likely to produce a flammable atmosphere, but a heated diesel tank or a spill onto hot plant is a different problem entirely.


**Teaching note:** Emphasise temperature dependence of vapour pressure. Link to tank fires and vapour cloud formation.

## Slide 20 · The Four Stages

**Section:** 06 · STAGES OF FIRE DEVELOPMENT  
**Subtitle:** Temperature and heat release rate through a compartment fire  
**Timecode:** 14:56 · 49 s


**Narration**

> This curve is the backbone of the lecture. Plot temperature against time in a compartment fire and four stages appear. First, ignition and the incipient stage, where the fire is small and local. Second, growth, where heat release rises, often in proportion to the square of time, while the hot layer deepens and descends. Third, the fully developed stage, where the fire is limited either by fuel supply or by ventilation and temperatures peak. Fourth, decay, as fuel is consumed or oxygen is exhausted, temperatures fall, but the fire is not necessarily out. Every stage has a signature that you can observe, and each one allows different tactics. The transitions between stages, not the stages themselves, are where firefighters are injured.

## Slide 21 · Stage 1 — Incipient

**Section:** 06 · STAGES OF FIRE DEVELOPMENT  
**Subtitle:** The first minutes decide the outcome  
**Timecode:** 15:46 · 46 s


**On screen**

- Fuel-limited, localised, often smouldering or small flame.
- Plume rises; a thin smoke layer begins to form at the ceiling.
- Temperatures in the layer: 50–150 °C.
- Detection, first-aid firefighting and evacuation are still possible.


**Narration**

> In the incipient stage the fire is small, localised, and usually fuel-limited. A plume rises from the burning object, strikes the ceiling and begins to spread as a thin layer. Upper-layer temperatures are modest, perhaps fifty to a hundred and fifty degrees, and the space below remains tenable. This is the stage in which automatic detection should operate, in which a trained occupant can use a portable extinguisher, and in which evacuation is straightforward. It is also the stage most often missed, because smoke production is small and the fire may be hidden inside a rack, a duct, a ceiling void or a piece of equipment. The most valuable fire dynamics skill in this stage is simply recognising that the fire exists.


**Teaching note:** Relate to detection design and to the value of early notification.

## Slide 22 · Stage 2 — Growth

**Section:** 06 · STAGES OF FIRE DEVELOPMENT  
**Subtitle:** The stage that kills: exponential energy, descending smoke  
**Timecode:** 16:33 · 50 s


**On screen**

- HRR rises approximately with the square of time.
- Ceiling jet spreads fire to distant combustibles.
- Hot layer descends, radiating down onto the fuel bed.
- Rollover appears; flashover conditions begin to build.


**Narration**

> Growth is the dangerous stage, and it is dangerous because it is non-linear. Heat release rises roughly with the square of time, so the fire that took two minutes to reach half a megawatt takes four minutes to reach two megawatts, and eight minutes to reach eight. The ceiling jet carries hot gas across the ceiling and can ignite combustible material far from the original fire. The hot layer thickens and descends. As it descends, it radiates down onto the fuel bed, which increases the burning rate, which in turn produces more hot gas. This is a positive feedback loop. Its visible signature is rollover: tongues of flame running through the smoke layer overhead. Rollover is a warning, not a guarantee, that flashover is approaching.


**Teaching note:** Use doubling logic explicitly: 2 minutes to 500 kW, 4 to 2 MW, 8 to 8 MW.

## Slide 23 · Stage 3 — Fully Developed

**Section:** 06 · STAGES OF FIRE DEVELOPMENT  
**Subtitle:** Peak energy, structural exposure, no survivable interior  
**Timecode:** 17:23 · 47 s


**On screen**

- Temperatures 800–1 200 °C in the gas phase.
- Burning rate controlled by ventilation or fuel, not by the fuel surface.
- Flames project from openings; structural steel is at risk.
- Interior operations are no longer survivable: defensive or transitional.


**Narration**

> In the fully developed stage the entire compartment is involved. Gas temperatures reach eight hundred to twelve hundred degrees, and the fire is no longer controlled by the surface area of the fuel. It is controlled either by the rate at which fuel is available or by the rate at which air can enter. Flames project from the openings, radiating intensely to any exposure. Structural steel, unprotected, reaches its critical temperature of about five hundred and fifty degrees within ten to twenty minutes of full involvement. At this point interior operations are not survivable, whatever the protective equipment. The tactical question becomes one of protecting exposures, cooling structural members from outside, and preventing spread, not of extinguishing the fire room.


**Teaching note:** Be explicit that PPE does not make a fully developed compartment survivable.

## Slide 24 · Stage 4 — Decay and Its Traps

**Section:** 06 · STAGES OF FIRE DEVELOPMENT  
**Subtitle:** A falling temperature is not an extinguished fire  
**Timecode:** 18:11 · 53 s


**On screen**

- Decay follows fuel consumption or oxygen depletion.
- Smouldering, hot embers and pyrolysis continue.
- Air entering a decayed, fuel-rich compartment can cause backdraft or reignition.
- Overhaul and atmospheric monitoring are fire dynamics tasks.


**Narration**

> Decay arrives in two forms. If fuel is consumed, the fire simply runs out of material and temperatures fall steadily. If oxygen is depleted first, the compartment becomes fuel-rich and the visible flame may die back, while the interior remains extremely hot and full of unburnt pyrolysis gases. That second form is a trap. A quiet, smoke-stained compartment with no visible flame may be ready to reignite violently the moment air enters. This is the physical basis of backdraft, which we will examine shortly. Decay is therefore not the end of the incident but a change of hazard, from heat and flame to toxic atmosphere, structural weakness and explosive reignition. Overhaul is a fire dynamics operation, not a tidying-up exercise.


**Teaching note:** Bridge to backdraft in section 12.

## Slide 25 · Typical Heat Release Rates

**Section:** 07 · HEAT RELEASE RATE  
**Subtitle:** Orders of magnitude every officer should carry in memory  
**Timecode:** 19:06 · 47 s


**On screen**

- Fire size spans six orders of magnitude.
- Warehouse rack fires: 5–30 MW per rack, higher with high-piled storage.
- Tank fires: 100 MW to several GW depending on diameter.
- Sprinkler and water supply design is based on these values.


**Narration**

> Fire size spans an enormous range. A wastebasket might peak at twenty to a hundred kilowatts. An upholstered armchair, two hundred to four hundred kilowatts. A passenger car, one and a half to five megawatts. A single retail rack of mixed goods, five to thirty megawatts. A large warehouse fire, fifty to a hundred megawatts or more. An open hydrocarbon tank fire, hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts depending on diameter. These figures are not academic. They determine sprinkler design, water supply, foam application rates and safe standoff distances. When an officer says the fire is 'big', a fire dynamics officer asks: how big, in megawatts, and what does that imply for the water we have?

## Slide 26 · How HRR Is Measured

**Section:** 07 · HEAT RELEASE RATE  
**Subtitle:** Oxygen consumption calorimetry: fire science's most robust instrument  
**Timecode:** 19:53 · 48 s


**On screen**

- Principle: 13.1 MJ is released per kg of oxygen consumed, for most fuels.
- Cone calorimeter: 100 × 100 mm samples at controlled heat flux.
- Furniture and room calorimeters: full-scale items, up to 10 MW.
- Field estimate: HRR from flame length, smoke volume or water flow.


**Narration**

> We measure heat release rate by measuring oxygen consumption. For almost all organic fuels, thirteen point one megajoules of energy are released per kilogram of oxygen consumed. So if we capture the effluent from a burning object, measure its flow and its oxygen concentration, and compare that with the incoming air, we can calculate the heat release rate directly. The cone calorimeter does this on small samples at a controlled heat flux. Furniture calorimeters do it on complete items. Large industrial calorimeters can measure ten megawatts or more. In the field we cannot measure oxygen consumption, so we estimate from observable proxies: flame length, smoke volume and density, the size of the burning area, and the water flow required to control it.


**Teaching note:** 13.1 MJ/kg O2 is worth memorising; it links combustion stoichiometry to calorimetry.

## Slide 28 · The Time-Squared Fire

**Section:** 08 · FIRE GROWTH  
**Subtitle:** A single parameter that describes the growth phase  
**Timecode:** 21:28 · 57 s


**On screen**

- Slow 0.00293 · Medium 0.01172 · Fast 0.0469 · Ultra-fast 0.1876
- Time to 1 055 kW: 600 s · 300 s · 150 s · 75 s.
- α is a property of the fuel package and its arrangement.


**Narration**

> The growth phase is commonly described by a single equation: heat release rate equals the growth coefficient alpha, multiplied by the square of time since ignition. Alpha has units of kilowatts per second squared, and standard values are used for design: slow, zero point zero zero two nine three; medium, zero point zero one one seven two; fast, zero point zero four six nine; and ultra-fast, zero point one eight seven six. The conventional benchmark is the time to reach one thousand and fifty-five kilowatts, which is one megawatt in British thermal units per second. That takes six hundred seconds for a slow fire, three hundred for medium, one hundred and fifty for fast and seventy-five for ultra-fast. Note what alpha really describes: not the material alone, but the fuel package, its arrangement and its ability to draw air.

## Slide 29 · Reading the Growth Curve

## Slide 30 · Where the t-Squared Model Fails

**Section:** 08 · FIRE GROWTH  
**Subtitle:** Use it for growth, not for the whole fire  
**Timecode:** 23:11 · 44 s


**On screen**

- It ignores fuel burnout and decay.
- It assumes unlimited fuel and air.

**Narration**

## Slide 31 · What Smoke Is Made Of

**Section:** 09 · SMOKE  
**Subtitle:** A toxic, hot, optically dense aerosol — and the main killer  
**Timecode:** 23:56 · 49 s


**On screen**

- Particulates: soot, ash, condensed hydrocarbons.
- Asphyxiants: carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide.
- Irritants: hydrogen chloride, acrolein, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides.
- Water vapour and unburnt fuel; temperature 300–700 °C in the layer.


**Narration**

> Smoke is not a single substance. It is an aerosol of solid particles, liquid droplets and gases. The particles are mainly soot, and soot is what makes smoke optically dense and what radiates heat so effectively. The gases include the asphyxiants carbon monoxide and hydrogen cyanide, the irritants hydrogen chloride, acrolein, sulphur dioxide and the nitrogen oxides, plus water vapour and unburnt fuel. In a compartment fire, most deaths are caused by inhalation of these gases, not by burns. Together they are synergistic: the combination is far more lethal than either alone.

## Slide 32 · Why Smoke Rises and Spreads

**Section:** 09 · SMOKE  
**Subtitle:** Buoyancy, expansion, and the paths of least resistance  
**Timecode:** 24:46 · 53 s


**On screen**

- Buoyancy: hot gas is less dense; the driving force is temperature difference.
- Expansion: smoke volume is roughly three times the air consumed.
- Drivers: stack effect, wind, HVAC, lift shafts, service risers.
- Leakage paths determine where smoke actually goes.


**Narration**

> Smoke rises because it is hot and therefore less dense than the surrounding air. The driving force is proportional to the temperature difference between the smoke and the ambient air, so a hotter fire produces a stronger plume. Second, combustion expands the gas. Burning one cubic metre of air produces roughly three cubic metres of smoke at flame temperature, so a fire acts as a pump, pushing gas out through every available opening. Third, the building imposes its own driving forces: stack effect in tall spaces, wind pressure on the facade, mechanical ventilation, and the buoyancy of hot gas in shafts and risers. The practical implication is that smoke finds the paths we forgot: cable penetrations, lift shafts, ceiling voids, and the gaps around poorly fitted fire doors.


**Teaching note:** Stack effect and leakage: link to smoke control design.

## Slide 33 · Stratification, the Smoke Layer and the Neutral Plane

**Section:** 09 · SMOKE  
**Subtitle:** Three terms that explain what you see at a doorway  
**Timecode:** 25:40 · 51 s


**Narration**

> In a compartment, hot smoke collects under the ceiling and forms a layer. Because hot gas is buoyant, it tends to stay above cooler air, and the boundary between the two is the interface. In an ideal two-layer model, above the interface it is hot and toxic; below it, the air remains relatively cool and clear. At an opening, such as a door, the pressure difference produces a two-way flow: hot gas flows out at the top, and fresh air flows in at the bottom. The height at which the flow reverses is the neutral plane. Reading the neutral plane is a direct measurement of fire conditions. A high neutral plane with a thin smoke layer means a small fire. A neutral plane near the floor means the compartment is full of hot gas, and the fire is either large or ventilation-limited and fuel-rich.


**Teaching note:** Ask: what does a neutral plane at head height tell you? Answers: significant fire, layer deep.

## Slide 34 · Smoke and Visibility: The Practical Currency

**Section:** 09 · SMOKE  
**Subtitle:** Optical density, visibility distance and tenability limits  
**Timecode:** 26:32 · 46 s


**On screen**

- Visibility in smoke: 3–10 m is typical in a developed fire.
- Tenability: 10 m visibility is a common design target.
- Smoke logged at 5 m means crews cannot find the fire or a exit.
- Thermal imaging restores some capability, but not all.


**Narration**

> For firefighters, the practical measure of smoke is visibility. Visibility depends on the optical density of the smoke, which depends on soot concentration. In a developed compartment fire, visibility of three to ten metres is typical; in a large volume with high soot yield, it can be less. Design guidance often uses ten metres as the minimum for a space to remain tenable. Below five metres, a crew cannot reliably find the seat of the fire, cannot see a casualty, and cannot confirm a exit. Thermal imaging cameras help, because they see through smoke to temperature differences, but they do not restore spatial orientation and they do not measure toxicity. This is why smoke conditions, not flame, usually set the pace of an interior attack.

## Slide 35 · Anatomy of a Fire Plume

**Section:** 10 · PLUMES & CEILING JETS  
**Subtitle:** Where the heat, the smoke and the danger actually go  
**Timecode:** 27:18 · 50 s


**Narration**

> A fire plume is the column of hot gas rising above the burning fuel. It has three regions. Directly above the fuel is the continuous flame zone, where the reaction is still completing. Above that is the intermittent flame zone, where flaming tongues appear and disappear, and where the temperature fluctuates violently. Above that is the buoyant plume, where the gas is hot but no longer burning, and where entrainment of surrounding air dominates. The plume entrains air continuously, so its mass flow increases with height, and its temperature decreases. The height of the flame, according to Heskestad, is zero point two three five times the heat release rate to the power two-fifths, minus about one diameter. Standing in a plume is never acceptable, even at ceiling level, because temperatures there can exceed six hundred degrees.


**Teaching note:** Heskestad flame height equation; give a numeric example verbally: 2 MW, 2 m diameter → ~4.5 m.

## Slide 36 · The Ceiling Jet

**Section:** 10 · PLUMES & CEILING JETS  
**Subtitle:** How a fire reaches the far end of a hall in seconds  
**Timecode:** 28:09 · 51 s


**On screen**

- Plume hits the ceiling and turns radially outward.
- Ceiling jet velocity and temperature decay with radius.
- Alpert: ΔT = 16.9 Q^(2/3) / H^(5/3) near the plume.
- Sprinklers and detectors are actuated by the ceiling jet.


**Narration**

> When the plume strikes a ceiling it cannot continue upward, so it turns and spreads radially as a ceiling jet. This jet is thin, fast and hot. Near the plume, Alpert's correlation gives the temperature rise as sixteen point nine, times heat release rate to the two-thirds, divided by the height to the five-thirds. Further out, the temperature rise falls as the two-thirds power of heat release rate divided by radius. The velocity follows a similar form. The ceiling jet is the reason a fire can activate a detector twenty metres away within a minute, and it is also the reason the first sprinkler to operate may be well away from the fire. In large-volume buildings the jet can travel a hundred metres or more, cooling and thickening as it goes, and creating the smoke reservoir that determines how fast the space fills.


**Teaching note:** Give the Alpert formula verbally; the equation card appears in section 18.

## Slide 37 · Building a Hot Layer

**Section:** 10 · PLUMES & CEILING JETS  
**Subtitle:** The feedback loop that leads to flashover  
**Timecode:** 29:01 · 55 s


**Narration**

> Now watch the layer build. The ceiling jet spreads, cools against the walls and the ceiling, and begins to descend. As the layer thickens, its underside radiates heat downward onto everything below: the fuel bed, the furniture, the racks, the people. That radiant feedback increases the rate of pyrolysis at the fuel surface, which increases the heat release rate, which produces more hot gas, which deepens the layer further. The layer also stores energy, so its temperature climbs. A layer at three hundred degrees radiates about five kilowatts per square metre to the floor; at six hundred degrees, about twenty. That number, twenty kilowatts per square metre at floor level, is one of the standard criteria for flashover. The layer is therefore not a consequence of the fire; it is part of the mechanism.

## Slide 38 · The Compartment as an Energy Balance

**Section:** 11 · COMPARTMENT FIRES  
**Subtitle:** Energy in equals energy stored plus energy lost  
**Timecode:** 29:56 · 45 s


**On screen**

- Heat generated by combustion = heat stored in gases and structure + heat lost through boundaries and openings.
- Boundaries lose heat by conduction and radiation; openings lose it by convection.
- Better insulation means a hotter, faster fire in the same compartment.
- A well-sealed compartment starves the fire of air and stores unburnt fuel.


**Narration**

> A compartment fire is an energy balance. The energy released by combustion must either be stored in the gases and the structure, or be lost through the boundaries and the openings. The boundaries lose heat by conduction into the walls and by radiation; the openings lose heat by convection, carrying hot gas out. This balance explains two results that are counter-intuitive at first. First, a well-insulated compartment produces a hotter fire than a poorly insulated one, because less energy escapes through the walls. Second, a tightly sealed compartment may look less fierce, because it is short of oxygen, while in fact it is storing unburnt fuel and enormous heat, waiting for a door to open.


**Teaching note:** Insulation paradox; sets up modern energy-efficient buildings and cold stores.

## Slide 39 · Ventilation: The Air Supply Sets the Ceiling

**Section:** 11 · COMPARTMENT FIRES  
**Subtitle:** The ventilation factor A√H and the mass flow through openings  
**Timecode:** 30:41 · 50 s


**On screen**

- Mass flow through an opening ≈ 0.5 A√H kg/s.
- A is opening area (m²), H is opening height (m).
- Ventilation-controlled HRR ≈ 1 500 A√H kW for typical fuels.
- Doubling a door's height raises airflow by about 41 %.


**Narration**

> The air supply through openings is described by the ventilation factor, A root H, where A is the area of the opening and H its height. The mass flow of air entering a compartment is roughly zero point five times A root H kilograms per second, and since about three megajoules are released per kilogram of air consumed, the maximum heat release rate for a ventilation-controlled fire is roughly fifteen hundred times A root H, in kilowatts. Notice that height matters as well as area: the height term creates the pressure difference that drives the flow. Doubling the height of an opening increases the airflow by about forty-one per cent, not by a hundred per cent, because the flow depends on the square root of height. This is a precise, usable relationship between a door and a fire's power.

## Slide 40 · Openings Change the Fire

**Section:** 11 · COMPARTMENT FIRES  
**Subtitle:** Ventilation-induced transitions and the danger of uncontrolled air  
**Timecode:** 31:32 · 50 s


**On screen**

- More air: burning rate rises, layer temperature rises, external flaming appears.
- Less air: fire becomes fuel-rich, produces more CO and unburnt gas.
- Opening a door can trigger flashover or backdraft.
- Ventilation must be coordinated with attack, never incidental.


**Narration**

> Openings change the fire in both directions. Provide more air and the burning rate rises, the layer temperature rises, and flames may begin to project from the opening as the fire becomes externally ventilated. Restrict air and the fire becomes fuel-rich: it produces more carbon monoxide, more unburnt hydrocarbons, and a darker, denser smoke, while the compartment stores energy. The dangerous transitions are in the middle. Opening a door to an under-ventilated fire can admit a sudden volume of air, which mixes with hot fuel-rich gas and can produce a flashover or a backdraft. That is why ventilation must be a deliberate, coordinated tactical action tied to the attack line, never an incidental consequence of where someone happened to walk.


**Teaching note:** Practical doctrine: vent for life, then for fire; coordinate with charged line.

## Slide 41 · Flashover

**Section:** 12 · FLASHOVER, BACKDRAFT & OTHER PHENOMENA  
**Subtitle:** The transition from a fire in a room to a room on fire  
**Timecode:** 32:22 · 48 s


**Narration**

> Flashover is the transition from a fire in a room to a room on fire. It is not a single instant but a rapid transition, typically lasting a few seconds to tens of seconds, in which the exposed surfaces in the compartment reach their ignition temperature almost simultaneously, and the fire spreads across the entire compartment. The standard indicators are an upper-layer gas temperature of about five hundred to six hundred degrees, a radiant heat flux at floor level of about twenty kilowatts per square metre, and flames issuing from the openings. Physically, it is the point at which the radiant feedback from the layer and the walls becomes sufficient to pyrolyse the whole fuel load at a rate the ventilation can support. After flashover, the fire is ventilation-controlled and the compartment is unsurvivable.


**Teaching note:** Define with three criteria; stress it is a transition, not an event with a timestamp.

## Slide 42 · Predicting Flashover

**Section:** 12 · FLASHOVER, BACKDRAFT & OTHER PHENOMENA  
**Subtitle:** Correlations, time to flashover, and honest uncertainty  
**Timecode:** 33:11 · 56 s


**On screen**

- Thomas: Q_FO ≈ 750 A√H (kW).
- McCaffrey: Q_FO ≈ 610 (h_k A_T A√H)^0.5 (kW).
- Combine with Q = α t² to estimate time to flashover.
- Predictions are ±50 %: use them for planning, not for entry decisions.


**Narration**

> Two classical correlations estimate the heat release rate needed to produce flashover. Thomas proposed seven hundred and fifty times A root H, in kilowatts. McCaffrey proposed six hundred and ten times the square root of the product of the effective heat transfer coefficient, the total enclosure area, and A root H. Both give similar answers for typical compartments, which is reassuring. For a room of twenty square metres with a single door, both predict flashover at about two megawatts. Combine that with a heat release rate of alpha t squared and you get a time to flashover: about seven minutes for a medium-growth fire, about three and a half minutes for a fast one. But be honest about uncertainty: these correlations carry errors of tens of per cent, and the real compartment has furniture, a non-uniform fuel load and a door that may be open or closed. Use them to plan, not to justify entry.


**Teaching note:** The honesty about uncertainty is important postgraduate content.

## Slide 43 · Backdraft

**Section:** 12 · FLASHOVER, BACKDRAFT & OTHER PHENOMENA  
**Subtitle:** A deflagration of accumulated fuel-rich gas when oxygen arrives  
**Timecode:** 34:07 · 56 s


**Narration**

> Backdraft is a different phenomenon from flashover, and confusing the two is dangerous. Backdraft requires a fire that has become severely under-ventilated, so that the compartment is hot and full of unburnt pyrolysis gases, with no visible flame. When an opening is made, air enters as a gravity current along the floor, mixes with the hot fuel-rich gas, and the mixture ignites, often at the ceiling. The result is a deflagration: a subsonic combustion wave that expands rapidly, pushing a fireball out of the opening with considerable pressure. The warning signs are a compartment that is hot but quiet, smoke pulsing in and out of gaps, discoloured or cracked windows, soot staining around openings, and smoke drawn inward when a door is cracked. The tactical rule is to vent from outside, from a protected position, and never to open a compartment while standing in the doorway.


**Teaching note:** Contrast flashover (fuel surfaces ignite) with backdraft (gas deflagration).

## Slide 44 · Other Phenomena You Must Recognise

**Section:** 12 · FLASHOVER, BACKDRAFT & OTHER PHENOMENA  
**Subtitle:** Rollover, smoke explosion, flash fire, boilover, BLEVE, dust explosion  
**Timecode:** 35:04 · 60 s


**On screen**

- Rollover: flames in the layer; precursor, not flashover.
- Smoke explosion: ignition of a pre-mixed smoke cloud outside the fire room.
- Boilover: water bottom in a tank turns to steam and ejects burning oil.
- BLEVE: vessel rupture with fireball, blast and fragments.
- Dust explosion: rapid deflagration of suspended combustible dust.


**Narration**

> Several related phenomena deserve a place in your memory. Rollover is the appearance of flames running through the hot layer; it is a warning of flashover, not flashover itself. A smoke explosion occurs when a pre-mixed cloud of unburnt gas and air, often in an adjacent space, is ignited. Flash fire is the ignition of a vapour cloud without significant overpressure, still lethal to anyone inside it. Boilover happens in tank fires when the water layer at the bottom of a tank is heated to boiling; the resulting steam expansion ejects burning oil over the rim, sometimes to a distance of ten tank diameters. A boiling liquid expanding vapour explosion, or BLEVE, occurs when a pressurised vessel containing liquid is heated until it ruptures, producing a fireball, a blast wave and fragments. And a dust explosion is a rapid deflagration of a suspended combustible dust, which can propagate through a plant in a chain of secondary explosions.

## Slide 45 · Two Regimes, Two Behaviours

**Section:** 13 · FUEL- AND VENTILATION-CONTROLLED FIRES  
**Subtitle:** The single most useful classification for tactical decision-making  
**Timecode:** 36:05 · 51 s


**On screen**

- Fuel-controlled: plenty of air, fire limited by fuel surface and arrangement.
- Ventilation-controlled: plenty of fuel, fire limited by air supply.
- Transition can be triggered by a door, a window, a roof, or a collapse.
- Regime determines temperature, CO production, flame behaviour and tactics.


**Narration**

> This classification is the most useful single idea in the lecture. A fuel-controlled fire has adequate air and is limited by the amount and arrangement of fuel. It burns efficiently, produces relatively less carbon monoxide, and its heat release rate follows the fuel's surface area. A ventilation-controlled fire has abundant fuel but insufficient air. It burns inefficiently, produces large quantities of carbon monoxide and unburnt fuel, and its heat release rate is set by the openings. The two regimes look different, sound different and behave differently under attack. In a fuel-controlled fire, water applied to the fuel reduces the heat release rate directly. In a ventilation-controlled fire, water may convert to steam before it reaches the fuel, and opening the compartment is the more powerful variable.

## Slide 46 · Diagnosing the Regime on the Fireground

**Section:** 13 · FUEL- AND VENTILATION-CONTROLLED FIRES  
**Subtitle:** Observable evidence, and what it licenses you to do  
**Timecode:** 36:56 · 49 s


**On screen**

- Bright, clean-burning flame with strong outward flow: fuel control.
- High CO and unburnt gas mean a smoke explosion or backdraft risk.

**Narration**

A fuel-controlled fire produces lighter, faster-moving smoke, bright flame, and a strong but steady outward flow at the openings. ## Slide 47 · Five Mechanisms of Fire Spread

**Section:** 14 · FIRE SPREAD IN INDUSTRIAL BUILDINGS  
**Subtitle:** How a fire escapes the room it started in  
**Timecode:** 37:46 · 51 s


**Narration**

> Fire spreads by five mechanisms, and industrial buildings offer opportunities for all five. Direct flame contact, where flames touch a fuel surface. Radiation, from the flame or from the hot layer, which preheats fuel at a distance. Convection, in which hot gas and embers travel through voids, ducts and openings. Conduction, along steel members, pipes and cable trays. And finally the transport of burning material itself: falling debris, burning liquid running to a low point, or conveyors carrying material. In a warehouse, radiation between racks and convection through the roof space usually dominate. In a process plant, burning liquid flow and jet fires dominate. Identifying which mechanism is active tells you which barrier to defend.

## Slide 48 · Vertical and Horizontal Spread

**Section:** 14 · FIRE SPREAD IN INDUSTRIAL BUILDINGS  
**Subtitle:** Racks, mezzanines, conveyor systems, shafts and cladding

## Slide 49 · Storage, Dust and Process Escalation

## Slide 50 · Reaction to Fire vs Fire Resistance

**Section:** 15 · MATERIALS, INSULATION AND STRUCTURE  
**Subtitle:** Two different questions about a building material  
**Timecode:** 40:26 · 56 s


**On screen**

- Reaction to fire: how does the material contribute to fire growth? (Classes A1–F)
- Fire resistance: how long does an element hold its function? (R, E, I)
- Combustible insulation can pass a fire resistance test and still spread fire.
- Both questions must be asked of every industrial system.


**Narration**

> Two different questions are asked of every building material. Reaction to fire asks how the material itself contributes to fire growth: how easily it ignites, how much heat it releases, how much smoke it produces. In the European classification system, classes run from A1 to F. A1 indicates no contribution to fire, while F means performance is not determined or does not meet class E. Fire resistance asks how long a building element continues to perform its function under specified fire exposure: its load-bearing capacity, its integrity, and its insulation value, denoted R, E and I. These are different assessments. A composite panel with a combustible core may achieve a fire resistance rating in a tested assembly while still containing combustible material. Always check the actual classification, assembly details and conditions of use.

## Slide 51 · Combustible Insulation and Composite Panels

**Section:** 15 · MATERIALS, INSULATION AND STRUCTURE  
**Subtitle:** Hidden voids, chimney effects and leapfrog fire spread  
**Timecode:** 41:23 · 56 s


**Narration**

> Insulated sandwich panels are used across industry for cold stores, clean rooms, warehouses and facades. They consist of two metal skins bonded to an insulating core, commonly polyisocyanurate, polyurethane, expanded polystyrene or mineral wool. Mineral wool cores are generally non-combustible; verify the classification of the complete product. Plastic cores can contribute fuel, but their behaviour varies substantially with chemistry, construction and fire testing. Some soften or melt, while others char. If fire reaches a combustible core through damaged joints or exposed edges, concealed fire spread may occur without obvious external flame. Metal facings can hide that spread and conduct heat. Fire may emerge far from its origin, so assess panel joints, penetrations, voids and tested system details rather than assuming all panels behave alike.


**Teaching note:** Explicitly state the mechanism: cavity chimney, melting core, delamination.

## Slide 55 · Why We Model Fire

**Section:** 17 · COMPUTATIONAL FIRE MODELLING  
**Subtitle:** Three families of model, three levels of fidelity  
**Timecode:** 45:05 · 58 s


**On screen**

- Empirical correlations: fast, transparent, narrow validity.
- Zone models (e.g. CFAST): two layers, fast, good for simple compartments.
- Field models (CFD, e.g. FDS): solve the flow field; detailed and expensive.
- All models are decision aids; none is a substitute for observation.


**Narration**

> Fire modelling exists because the governing equations cannot be solved by hand for a real building. At the simplest level are empirical correlations: Alpert's ceiling jet, Heskestad's plume, Thomas's flashover criterion. They are fast, transparent and useful within their validated range. Next are zone models, such as CFAST, which divide a compartment into an upper hot layer and a lower cool layer and solve the mass and energy balance between them. They are fast and useful for suitable geometries. At the top are field models or computational fluid dynamics, such as FDS, which divide the space into many computational cells and solve conservation equations for mass, momentum, energy and species. They are powerful and computationally demanding. Their credibility depends on input data, appropriate assumptions, verification, validation and sensitivity analysis.


**Teaching note:** Introduce the idea of validation and uncertainty.

## Slide 56 · Inside a CFD Fire Model

**Section:** 17 · COMPUTATIONAL FIRE MODELLING  
**Subtitle:** Large-eddy simulation, radiation and the resolution question  
**Timecode:** 46:04 · 64 s


**On screen**

- Solves conservation of mass, momentum, energy and species on a grid.
- Turbulence modelled with large-eddy simulation.
- Radiation solved by a finite-volume or discrete-ordinate method.
- Grid size is tied to the characteristic fire diameter D*.
- Output: temperature, velocity, visibility, species, heat flux.


**Narration**

> A computational fluid dynamics fire model solves the conservation equations of mass, momentum, energy and chemical species on a three-dimensional grid. Turbulence is often handled by large-eddy simulation, which resolves the large eddies and models smaller scales. Radiation is solved separately, often with a finite-volume method. Grid resolution is a critical practical question. The characteristic fire diameter, D-star, provides a useful scale for assessing resolution. Ratios of D-star to cell size between four and sixteen occur in many validation studies, but they are not a universal acceptance criterion. Check the relevant validation evidence and demonstrate mesh sensitivity for the outputs that matter. Too coarse a grid can smear the plume; finer grids increase computational cost without automatically guaranteeing accuracy. Outputs include temperature, velocity, visibility and species concentration, with uncertainty that must be assessed before using them in design or investigation.

## Slide 57 · Using Models Without Being Fooled by Them

**Section:** 17 · COMPUTATIONAL FIRE MODELLING  
**Subtitle:** Validation, sensitivity and the operational use of simulation  
**Timecode:** 47:08 · 53 s


**On screen**

- Check the model against a real fire or a benchmark experiment.
- Vary the uncertain inputs; report a range, not a number.
- Operational uses: pre-incident plans, training, reconstruction, design.
- Never let a model override a fireground observation.


**Narration**

> The professional discipline in modelling is not running the model; it is knowing when to trust it. First, verification: are the equations implemented and solved correctly? Models support pre-incident planning, engineered fire protection, training scenarios and incident reconstruction. They do not establish that entry into a burning building is safe. > Four families of tools support assessment. Thermal imaging cameras show apparent surface temperatures and thermal contrasts within their operating limits. They cannot see through opaque walls or metal skins, and reflections, emissivity, smoke, water and viewing angle can mislead. They do not measure hidden gas temperature or certify structural safety. Smoke observation provides clues about movement, pressure and changing conditions, but colour alone cannot identify fuel, temperature or toxicity. Velocity, density, direction and pulsing must be interpreted together with ventilation and other evidence; no single sign diagnoses the fire regime reliably. Modelling supports pre-planning, design and training with stated uncertainty. Monitoring, including suitable gas detection and remote imagery, adds information but has coverage and instrument limitations. All these tools support competent judgement; none provides a standalone safe-entry indication.


**Teaching note:** Be critical about TIC limitations: emissivity, reflections, range.

## Slide 75 · A Size-Up Sequence Built on Physics

**Section:** 21 · TOOLS FOR FIRE DYNAMICS ASSESSMENT

**On screen**

- Fuel: commodity, arrangement, dust, vapour and reactivity.
- Smoke: observe trends; single signs are not diagnostic.

- Air: assess changing ventilation and flow paths.
- Assessment: communicate uncertainty; continually reassess.


**Narration**

> A physics-informed size-up considers the building, fuel, smoke, heat and air movement, then records a provisional assessment. Identify construction, openings, concealed spaces and potentially combustible panels. Establish commodity hazards, storage arrangement and the possibility of dust, vapour or reactive materials. Observe smoke movement and changing conditions without treating colour or layer height as a definitive diagnosis. Use thermal imaging within its limitations and do not approach closer simply to feel radiant heat. Consider ventilation flow paths, suppression systems, water supplies, life risks and structural hazards. Communicate what is known, what is uncertain and what changes would trigger reassessment or withdrawal. Size-up is continuous, not a ninety-second test that guarantees safety for the rest of an incident. Apply local procedures and competent incident command throughout.
