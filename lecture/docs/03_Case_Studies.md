# IF 701 · Fire Dynamics — Industrial Case Studies

**Master in Industrial Firefighting · Triune Biblical University Global Extension, USA**

Three incidents are analysed in depth. Each is chosen because it isolates one physical mechanism that recurs across industry: a **distributed dust fuel**, a **flammable vapour cloud**, and a **combustible cladding system**. The purpose is not to narrate tragedy but to practise mechanism analysis: identify the fuel, the heat transfer path, the ventilation condition, and the point at which the outcome became determined.

## Case 1 — West Pharmaceutical Services, Kinston, North Carolina, 29 January 2003

### What happened
A deflagration occurred in a production area of a pharmaceutical manufacturing plant where a rubber and plastic compound was processed. The initial event was followed by a series of secondary explosions that propagated through the building. Six people died, dozens were injured, and the facility was destroyed. The U.S. Chemical Safety Board investigated and identified the accumulation of combustible polyethylene dust as the central fuel hazard.

### Fire dynamics analysis

**Fuel.** Fine polyethylene dust had settled on structural beams, ledges, equipment and above a suspended ceiling. A dust layer is a *distributed* fuel load: it has an enormous surface area relative to its mass, it is easily lofted, and it sits in places that are never inspected.

**The layer-to-cloud transition.** A settled layer is not, by itself, explosive — the burning velocity of a dust layer is low. The hazard appears when the layer is disturbed: by a primary event, by a pressure wave, by cleaning, or by a falling object. The disturbance creates a suspended cloud within the flammable range, and a cloud deflagrates with a flame speed of metres per second, generating overpressure.

**Propagation.** A deflagration in one room raises pressure, which vents through doors, ducts and penetrations, lofting more dust in adjacent spaces. The result is a chain of secondary explosions, each often larger than the last. This is why the CSB found that the secondary events caused most of the destruction.

**Ventilation and confinement.** Confinement converts the same combustion into pressure. The building's compartments, the suspended ceiling void, and the ductwork all acted as confining volumes with limited vent area.

### Lessons for the industrial firefighter
1. A dust layer is a fuel load, and a layer is a fuse. Where a layer exists, a cloud is one disturbance away.
2. Suspended ceilings and voids are fuel reservoirs, not just services spaces.
3. Secondary explosions are frequently more destructive than the initiating event; expect propagation through every opening.
4. Pre-incident plans for dust-handling occupancies must include housekeeping standards, dust layer depth limits, and ignition-source control — not merely extinguisher locations.
5. At an incident, dust deposits that have been disturbed by an explosion remain a re-ignition and secondary-explosion hazard; do not use forced ventilation or high-pressure streams in a dust-laden space without specialist advice.

## Case 2 — Buncefield Oil Storage Terminal, Hemel Hempstead, UK, 11 December 2005

### What happened
A large petrol storage tank (Tank 912) was overfilled during a transfer. Petrol cascaded down the tank shell and formed a large flammable vapour cloud in cold, still, stable atmospheric conditions. The cloud ignited and produced a vapour cloud explosion with widespread blast damage; 43 people were injured, and there were no direct fatalities in the explosion. The ensuing fire involved more than twenty tanks and burned for several days.

### Fire dynamics analysis

**Flash point and vapour pressure.** Petrol's flash point is around −43 °C. At ambient temperature the liquid is always producing a flammable vapour above its surface; the lower explosive limit (about 1.4 % by volume) is easily reached in a spill. The hazard therefore exists **before** any flame.

**Dispersion.** Cold, still air with a temperature inversion suppressed vertical mixing, so the vapour travelled as a dense, low-lying cloud. Dispersion is a fire dynamics problem: it depends on atmospheric stability, wind, topography and the physical form of the release (a cascading liquid produces an aerosol, not a pure vapour).

**Confinement and overpressure.** A vapour cloud explosion requires some congestion or confinement to accelerate the flame and generate pressure. Process pipework, bunds, buildings and tankage provided that congestion.

**Escalation.** Once alight, radiation between tanks drove the multi-tank fire. Tank spacing, bunding, drainage and cooling water capacity are the controls that prevent a single tank fire from becoming a terminal fire.

### Lessons for the industrial firefighter
1. The most dangerous phase of a tank farm incident may precede any visible fire. Treat an overfill or a large spill as an incident in progress.
2. Foam is a *vapour-suppression* tool. Its value is highest before ignition and immediately after extinguishment; it is not a cure for an established tank fire without adequate application rate and supply.
3. Radiation between tanks is predictable: use the point-source estimate to determine cooling priorities and safe positioning.
4. Bunds and drainage are fire dynamics controls; a burning liquid that enters a drain reappears somewhere else.
5. Site emergency plans must integrate the terminal operator, the fire service, and neighbouring installations, because the hazard does not respect the fence line.

## Case 3 — Grenfell Tower, London, 14 June 2017

### What happened
Fire spread rapidly up the external facade of a 24-storey residential building, from the fourth floor to the roof in a period measured in minutes. Seventy-two people died. The cladding system comprised aluminium composite panels with a polyethylene core, together with combustible polyisocyanurate insulation, over the original concrete frame.

### Fire dynamics analysis

**Reaction to fire versus fire resistance.** The cladding system could satisfy certain fire-resistance tests while still being highly combustible in its core. This distinction — how a material contributes to fire growth versus how long an element retains its function — is the key technical point.

**Cavity chimney.** The cladding was separated from the structure by a ventilated cavity. Once fire entered the cavity, buoyancy drew air in at the base and hot gas rose, heating the polyethylene core above it. The cavity acted as a chimney, and the chimney accelerated spread.

**Melting and falling debris.** Polyethylene melts and drips. Burning material fell away from the facade, igniting lower levels, balconies and ground-level items, and creating new ignition points below the fire front — "leapfrog" spread.

**Window failure and internal spread.** As the facade heated, glazing failed and fire entered flats, producing internal spread in addition to external.

**The industrial parallel.** Cold stores, clean rooms, warehouses and process buildings are commonly clad or lined with metal-skinned sandwich panels with polyisocyanurate, polyurethane or expanded polystyrene cores. The physics is identical: a combustible core concealed between metal skins; a cavity that can act as a chimney; combustion that is invisible from outside until it emerges far from the origin.

### Lessons for the industrial firefighter
1. Ask what is *inside* the wall. The external appearance of a building tells you very little about its fire behaviour.
2. Where plastic-cored panels are present, treat external spread as a primary hazard: expect fire to appear at high level and at a distance from the seat.
3. Do not assume a "fire-resisting" wall is a barrier: check its reaction-to-fire classification and the combustibility of any core.
4. Falling debris creates new fire fronts and hazards to crews below; cordon accordingly.
5. Post-incident, the fire-load and cavity pathways are the evidence that matters: record them before remediation.

## Structured case analysis template

Students should answer in this order. Marks are awarded for the mechanism, not for narrative detail.

1. **Building and enclosure** — volume, geometry, openings, compartmentation, cladding and insulation.
2. **Fuel** — type, distribution, arrangement, quantity; is it a layer, a pool, a cloud, a rack, or a liquid?
3. **Ignition and early growth** — piloted or autoignition; likely α class; time to the first visible fire.
4. **Heat transfer** — which mode dominated at each stage; what preheated what.
5. **Ventilation and regime** — fuel-controlled or ventilation-controlled; where did the air come from?
6. **Smoke and toxicity** — production rate, movement path, tenability.
7. **Escalation** — the specific mechanism that turned a local fire into a site emergency.
8. **Decision points** — the three moments at which a different action could have changed the outcome.
9. **Transferable lesson** — one sentence that applies to any similar site.
